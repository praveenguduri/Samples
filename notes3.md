Introduction
Virtually every company possesses the building blocks necessary to create effective analytical environments: comprehensive source data, databases, Extraction-Transformation-Load (ETL) tools, capable technology professionals, and business subject matter experts. But few companies have high-performance data environments. Why?
Obstacle 1: Lack of Self-Service and Disparate Results
Virtually all organizations evaluate their health and effectiveness via a set of key performance indicators (KPI’s). The expression and assembly of these calculations, however, is often fraught with inconsistency. The nuances necessary to properly calculate each value (e.g., which elements to combine, how to combine, etc.) are not broadly understood nor efficiently governed. Moreover, the execution of these calculations may require technical sophistication not possessed by the average user/analyst. Thus, the enterprise is presented with myriad figures and incongruent results. The subsequent lack of “truth” requires the organization to waste valuable time reconciling differences rather than acting upon the insights the KPI’s are meant to deliver.
Solution: Establish a Semantic Layer / Feature Store
Semantic Layers are a critical architectural component that help overcome chronic calculation inconsistencies and assembly challenges. Wikipedia describes semantic layers as follows:
“A semantic layer is a business representation of corporate data that helps end users access data autonomously using common business terms. A semantic layer maps complex data into familiar business terms such as product, customer, or revenue to offer a unified, consolidated view of data across the organization.
By using common business terms, rather than data language, to access, manipulate, and organize information, a semantic layer simplifies the complexity of business data. Business terms are stored as objects in a semantic layer, which are accessed through business views.
The semantic layer enables business users to have a common "look and feel" when accessing and analyzing data stored in relational databases and OLAP cubes. This is claimed to be core business intelligence (BI) technology that frees users from IT while ensuring correct results.
Business Views is a multi-tier system that is designed to enable companies to build comprehensive and specific business objects that help report designers and end users access the information they require.
Feature Stores are a unique type of semantic solution designed to assist data scientists. Like semantic layers, Feature Stores offer central, consistent, virtualized expressions of critical calculations. However, Feature Stores specifically package this content for efficient use within data science models. Regardless of their respective uses, both types of semantic solutions effectively centralize the execution of critical calculations and require less technical skill to access.
Obstacle 2: Data Is Not Properly Validated, Transformed, and Integrated
The semantic solutions described above offer tremendous analytical efficiencies by pre-joining underlying data structures and centrally executing calculations. However, semantic solutions become extremely complex and inefficient when they attempt to convert organic source data into more useful and reliable forms. Further, the data feeding the semantic layer may be volatile, difficult to understand, fraught with data quality issues, and difficult to access. Here, “access” is not merely about one’s ability to securely reach and engage with the content. Rather, “access” refers to one’s ability to gracefully and confidently navigate the labyrinthine relationships and myriad elements that comprise corporate systems. Foundationally, source system data is frequently organized to facilitate speedy transaction processing, efficient input, and/or minimized storage. As a result, data may span thousands of structures with opaque relationships and cumbersome forms. Though the semantic layers may be able to pre-join these structures, they cannot execute the acrobatics necessary to recast oddly built content into durable, analysis-friendly forms.
Without the proper support architecture, users seeking to unlock insights within their data face daunting tasks:
·       Locating the data
·       Ascertaining the meaning of the data
·       Removing redundant data
·       Reconciling conflicting data
·       Decomposing overloaded data attributes
·       Determining how to relate the structures containing the data
·       Restructuring the content to reduce the number of data assets and simplify the data landscape
Even if the preceding challenges are overcome, the resulting deliverables are frequently vulnerable. When individual reports, analyses, models, etc., draw directly from organic source content, they must be refactored if (when!) new content emerges. If existing systems introduce new schemas and/or new systems are acquired, the calculation, integration, and transformation tactics undertaken by each deliverable will likely require remediation.
Solution: Establish A Curated Data Layer (i.e., Domain Integrated Layer)
Within a curated data layer (a.k.a. conformed layer, Domain Integrated Structures), raw source data are cleansed, transformed, and mastered into stable, consumable, user-friendly[  structures. These structures offer a method of defining and managing critical data by providing a single, integrated point of reference. Rather than portraying data as they exist within the source systems, the curated layer presents data in an ideal, elemental, and business-oriented manner. The result: high-quality and easily consumed content. In Discover parlance, the curated data are referred to as the Domain Integrated Structures. These structures simplify the work related to building and maintaining the semantic definitions. The optimized, highly trusted content within allows end users to construct a variety of consumer data products in a self-directed manner, without the involvement of technical resources. As a result, data products can be developed much quicker without sacrificing quality.
Notably, the Domain Integrated Structures insulate users and deliverables against defects. Defective data is cleansed en route to the Domain Integrated Structures and related messages are written to a central data quality log. Unknown and missing data are similarly identified en route, messaged, and clearly distinguished within the Domain structures.
Despite overcoming the two preceding obstacles, the organization still requires a rich and reliable source of enterprise data that is tracked historically. There are many data collection strategies that are currently en vogue, including Data Lakes and Data Vaults.
Data Lakes
Data Lakes are general repositories in which data is stored in its natural format. Data objects may be files, database structures, blobs, etc. The goal of the Data Lake is to offer a single storage location for all enterprise data to support analytics and data visualization. Often Data Lakes utilize non-database, “big data” solutions (e.g., Hadoop, S3) to accommodate and process unstructured data.
On its face, the construction of a Data Lake seems like an intuitive and practical first step. However, many Data Lakes become dumping grounds and data graveyards in which undocumented data is recklessly deposited with the hope of supporting some undefined, future use. Typically, these efforts focus on the mere collection of data, without giving the necessary regard to the completeness, understanding, and consistent historical management of content. Further, the approach may offer insignificant regard for the compliant and responsible treatment of personally identifiable information (e.g., customer contact details, addresses, tax identification numbers, etc.; “PII”).
Data Vaults
Data Vaults utilize a database-oriented method for modeling raw data. Like Data Lakes, Data Vaults seek to centralize the storage of raw, enterprise data. However, Data Vaults embrace a more structured approach to storing content. Specifically, Data Vaults place pronounced emphasis on tracking and storing all changes in the raw source data. To do this, the approach must transform the structure of incoming data into normalized Hub and Satellite structures. The Hub structures centralize the collection of entity keys and assigned surrogate keys. The various attributes that describe the keys (that may change over time) are stored in Satellite structures and can be related back to the Hub structures via surrogate keys. Data Vault’s emphasis on the responsible, detailed storage of all historical changes in the raw data is laudable. However, its implementation can be quite complex and subject to implementation mistakes that alter the true representation of the raw data. Particularly, this approach requires the development of involved architectural processes to transform the data into the Hub and Satellite structures. Further, detailed delta detection processes must be created to isolate and store the changes within incoming data. The result: myriad structure joins that often prohibit efficient end-user access.
Solution: Establish A Source Data Warehouse
The Discover methodology embraces the best features of the preceding two techniques by establishing a Source Data Warehouse. Like Data Vaults, the Source Data Warehouse will store enterprise content in a database format. Further, the Source Data Warehouse will seek to accumulate and preserve historical data. However, the Source Data Warehouse will not utilize elaborate normalization and transformation to store this historical content. In this sense, the approach is more akin to Data Lakes as data will be stored in its natural form. The ingesting process can be automated so the inclusion of new data into the source data warehouse can be accomplished within hours without sacrificing appropriate data management principles. Incoming data will be subject to historical data mastering, but they will continue to exist largely within their organic structures. Unlike Data Lakes, however, the Source Data Warehouse will execute light validation (i.e., data type validation) and place a pronounced emphasis on metadata. Data placed within the Source Data Warehouse will be judiciously selected and thoroughly profiled and documented (including business metadata, referential integrity rules, valid domain values, tokenization candidacy, etc.) prior to being incorporated into the Domain Integrated Structures.
Co-locating all enterprise source data within a central repository allows users to see all analytical possibilities. It also provides a broad and fertile source of content for the downstream Domain Integrated Structures. Most importantly, the Source Data Warehouse acts as a convenient, accessible repository for stop-gap reporting. Data not yet included in the Domain Integrated Structures (e.g., newly added source data, existing but newly populated data, etc.) can still be brought to bear in downstream analysis. By simply joining the raw data in the Source Data Warehouse to the Domain Integrated Structures and/or including such joins in the semantic layer, users are assured that all data can be conveniently exploited.
